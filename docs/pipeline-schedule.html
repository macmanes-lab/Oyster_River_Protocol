The four concurrency decisions

  1. Stage A: Trinity phase 1 vs. SPAdes55/SPAdes75 (always on). Phase 1 is largely insensitive to its CPU share above Inchworm's own --inchworm_cpu=10 cap, so it only needs TRINITY_PHASE1_SHARE (25%) of the CPU/mem budget; SPAdes -- fast, but does scale with cores -- gets the rest. Each SPAdes assembly is followed immediately by its own DIAMOND search.
  2. Stage B: Trinity phase 2 vs. Trans-ABySS (always on). Phase 2 (thousands of independent per-gene-component ParaFly jobs, by far Trinity's dominant cost -- around 34h on the SRR1789336 benchmark) gets TRINITY_PHASE2_SHARE (95%) of CPU; Trans-ABySS gets the rest, since its own dominant cost -- the initial FASTQ read + De Bruijn graph build -- runs single-threaded no matter how many cores it's given (traced to abyss-pe falling through to the plain, unthreaded ABYSS binary; see NOTES.md 2026-08-19). Its mem share isn't cut the same way, since its memory footprint doesn't shrink with fewer cores. Starting Phase 2 here rather than after Trans-ABySS finishes reclaims what was previously idle Trinity-lane time -- not yet validated on a real run.
  3. orthofuser_branch alongside merge_branch. Both only need short_fastas, so they're independent until they join at makeorthout. This is the branch pair behind the test37/test36 timing delta you looked at earlier — each branch only took ~5s on the sample dataset, so the concurrency saved seconds there, but ~5h11m on the full SRR1789336 benchmark.
  4. transrate alongside strandeval. Cheap and independent, so they still run as a small concurrent pair even though BUSCO right before them deliberately does not share its CPU budget with anything — it dwarfs both in wall time.
