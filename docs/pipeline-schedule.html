Oyster Pipeline Schedule

Every box below is a real self.step() call in the order main() issues it, run as it actually executes at --max-parallel 2. The pipeline is a long sequential chain, broken by four concurrent stages — two always on regardless of the flag, two switched on by it.

Reflects oyster.py on master at ORP 4.1.0-dev9 (the two-track merge; the OrthoFinder merge was removed). chowder.py runs this same graph from run_filtershort down, with one ingest step in place of the assembler stages — see pipeline-steps.md.

The concurrency decisions

  1. Trans-ABySS lane vs. Trinity lane (always on). Trans-ABySS gets TRANSABYSS_SHARE (40%) of --cpu and TRANSABYSS_MEM_SHARE (25%) of --mem for the whole assembly stage and starts at once: its dominant cost, the initial FASTQ read + De Bruijn graph build, runs single-threaded no matter how many cores it's given (abyss-pe falls through to the plain, unthreaded ABYSS binary; see NOTES.md 2026-08-19), so at ~4.5h on SRR1789336 it is now longer than all of Trinity with an OpenMP ParaFly. The Trinity lane gets the rest. Each assembly is followed immediately by its own DIAMOND search.
  2. Inside the Trinity lane: Stage A, then Stage B. Stage A runs Trinity phase 1 beside spadesauto/spadeshigh, splitting the lane's cores evenly (TRINITY_PHASE1_SHARE) and its mem 25/75 (TRINITY_PHASE1_MEM_SHARE, since SPAdes treats --memory as a hard cap); phase 1 has been the slower of the two. Stage B runs phase 2 -- thousands of independent per-gene-component ParaFly jobs -- on the whole lane once both are done.
  3. The remaining diamond passes beside pool_branch. The two-track selection needs every assembly's Swiss-Prot hits as well as the pool's scores, and the two are independent until twotrack_select. Under oyster.py the assembler lanes have already run three of the four searches, so only Trinity's is left (its lane has only just finished); under chowder.py none have run. score_pool (pyTransRate over the pool, hours) keeps all of --cpu; the diamond passes run beside it on a quarter of --cpu, with a quarter of --mem (at most 16 GB) taken out of pytransrate's budget.
  4. strandeval beside the final pyTransRate scoring. strandeval takes minutes and the pytransrate step over the finished assembly much longer, so splitting the cores evenly, which ORP did through 4.1.0-dev8, left strandeval's half idle for most of pyTransRate's run. pyTransRate now keeps every core and strandeval runs on a quarter of --cpu, at most 8 threads. BUSCO just before them shares its budget with nothing.

One thing this diagram deliberately doesn't show

Gzipping the files a finished run keeps — the corrected read pair and the four individual assemblies — isn't a step and has no place in the chain above. compress_async() queues each file on a two-worker background pool the moment the step that writes it returns: the corrected pair right after run_rcorrector, so it compresses underneath the assembler lanes, and each assembly as its own assembler finishes. That's much earlier than the pipeline is finished reading those files (c1/c2 are read through to strandeval at the very bottom), which is why the originals stay in place until cleanup — by which point there is nothing left to compress and the last node is a series of unlinks. The one place the pool is ever waited on is cleanup itself, which refuses to delete an original whose .gz didn't finish.

Nor does it show what makes the chain resumable. A step is skipped when its outputs exist and are newer than its inputs, and each step touches reports/.<run>.<step>.running before it starts and removes it when it succeeds. A step that was killed or failed partway is therefore re-run on the next invocation, rather than trusted on the strength of a half-written output.
